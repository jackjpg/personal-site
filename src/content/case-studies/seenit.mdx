<Section>

As founding designer at Seenit, I explored whether screenshots could become a new social primitive. Over three weeks, we launched a beta to 10 friendship groups, generating ~200 cards with a 20–30% share rate. The goal was to turn an existing, non-performative behaviour into a social update between friends.

</Section>

<Section title="Background">

Around the time people were posting ChatGPT’s “read” on themselves, I noticed that people love funny AI interpretations of themselves. That led me to a question: what behaviour shows who we are without requiring performance? Screenshots were the obvious answer.

Screenshots are a high-signal behavioural trace: they capture intent without requiring performance. Unlike photos or posts, they're taken for the self, which makes them a rare input for honest expression.

</Section>

<Section title="Problem: Friends don't feel seen unless they speak up">

Most of what shapes our days now happens online. We rarely message about these things, but they say a lot about where our heads are at. As a result, staying close increasingly requires speaking up or performing presence, and when no one does, friendships go quiet even when nothing is wrong.

</Section>

<SectionWithMedia title="Opportunity: Let friends see what we’re up to online">

Screenshots already capture what we're thinking about, worrying about, and circling. If interpreted correctly, they could become a lightweight way for friends to stay in sync without requiring messaging or performing. To understand whether this could sustain a real social loop, I defined three behavioural questions:

1. Expression: can AI turn mundane screenshots into something people want to share?

2. Reaction: does the output spark replies or conversation in existing channels?

3. Usage: can this become a repeated behaviour, not a novelty?

Together, these framed the core bet: Can screenshots power response-driven sharing between friends?

</SectionWithMedia>

<Section title="First bet: Weekly summaries">

We needed to know if the system could reliably land on something people would actually send. If the output wasn’t specific, surprising, or funny, people wouldn’t share it. The big unlock came when I defined a set of gold-standard cards and built a scoring rubric around them. 

</Section>

<Section>

To test the riskiest assumptions quickly, I partnered with an engineer to ship a V1: A Sunday card summarising your week’s screenshots. Whether people actually shared the cards was the fastest way to test if screenshots carried any social signal at all. 

I kept V1 intentionally simple to answer three questions the product hinged on: (1) would people share an AI interpretation of their week? (2) which tone would they choose? and (3) were people comfortable sharing screenshots with a system, given how private they usually are?

<Section title="Learnings from weekly summaries">

From ~200 generated cards, a few clear patterns emerged. The strongest engagement came from people already active in small friendship groups. Meme-like captions consistently outperformed factual summaries, and sharing happened primarily in WhatsApp groups of 5–12 people (not IG), where cards often triggered group discussion. People cared less about posting their own card than about seeing what their friends were sharing.

<V3Image
  src="/Project_seenit/user_feedback.png"

What didn’t work was the weekly cadence. WAU dropped ~20% week over week, and 7 out of 10 interviewees said that by the time the Sunday card arrived, they’d already moved on from the moments it described. People responded to immediacy and presence.

</Section>

<Section title="Design explorations">

At this point, the design challenge shifted. Instead of asking how do we summarise behaviour?, I began exploring how can screenshots create the feeling of being seen? This led to three distinct directions that tested a different way screenshots could show up socially: as reactions, signals, or presence.

</Section>

  src="/Project_seenit/Design_exploration_Seenit.png"

<Section>

1. Interpretive stickers: highly shareable, but functioned as reactions, not an ongoing social loop

2. Shared signals: socially interesting, but uneven participation created imbalance

3. Live signals: the only direction that consistently increased the feeling of closeness in real time

</Section>

<Section title="Lowering the cost of sharing">

After prototyping all three directions, I moved forward with live signals as as the direction most aligned with immediacy and presence.

Adding a status was the primary input into the Seenit loop, so reducing friction here was critical. Screenshotting is already an instinctive behaviour, and the product needed to sit on top of that action rather than ask users to context-switch into posting.

I explored multiple triggering mechanisms, including automatic uploads via iOS shortcuts. Due to OS limitations, I designed a passive interaction pattern using the Action Button (or Back Tap) combined with Live Activities. This reduced the cost of sharing enough that statuses felt closer to notifications than posts.

</Section>

<Section title="System experience">

Because statuses were generated by an LLM, misinterpretation and oversharing were inevitable edge cases. 

Rather than treating this as an editing problem, I designed regeneration as a silent escape hatch. With a single tap, users could discard a status and generate a new one before it became visible to friends.

Regeneration gives users confidence that they aren't locked into what the system inferred, making it easier to share passively and more often.

</Section>

<Section title="Viewer experience">

Statuses appear chronologically and are only replaced when a user shares again. There are no likes, reshares, or algorithmic ranking.



This was a deliberate choice for both UX and versioning. It allowed the product to function as an awareness layer rather than a feed.



To explore how conversation might start without introducing complexity, I experimented with LLM-generated reactions. These were derived directly from the status itself and surfaced as simple prompts, rather than explicit "likes" or comments. Tapping a reaction opened WhatsApp with a pre-filled message and a thumbnail of the status, keeping the interaction anchored in existing group chats.  The goal was to lower the barrier of responding.

</Section>

<Section title="Identity & Avatars">

Photos and faces play a disproportionate role in social recognition. Seenit generates stylised avatars from a user's IG photo using a diffusion model. These avatars became consistent identity markers across the feed, widgets, invites, and statuses.

I explored dynamically changing avatar expressions based on each generated status. While visually compelling, this increased system complexity and was cut for V1 in favour of stability.

</Section>

<Section title="Learnings">

1. Emotional intent beats task completion. Social LLM outputs should surface what the user is already feeling.

2. Tone is the interface. Users judge accuracy emotionally, not factually.

3. Specificity creates trust. Concrete cues (“£259 satisfy vest”) outperform abstraction.

4. Prompts are systems. Structure, scoring, and gold standards drove consistency more than iteration.

5. Presence > identity. Friends care more about what you’re in the middle of than how you present yourself.

</Section>
